    Lecture 4¶

    PID Control System¶

    Overview¶

    In this lecture, we introduce one of the most widely used controllers in control systems:

    • Proportional (P)
    • Integral (I)
    • Derivative (D)

    Together, they form the PID controller.

    We will discuss: - why PID is needed - what each term does - how PID affects system behavior - a small worked example - Ziegler–Nichols tuning - conceptual quiz and short questions

    1) Why Do We Need PID?¶

    From the previous lecture, we already know that a feedback system may still suffer from:

    • slow response
    • overshoot
    • oscillation
    • steady-state error

    So, even if a system uses feedback, it may not behave the way we want.

    A PID controller helps improve: - speed - accuracy - stability

    2) PID Control Law¶

    The standard PID control law in time domain is:

    \[ u(t) = K_p e(t) + K_i \int_0^t e(\tau)\, d\tau + K_d \frac{de(t)}{dt} \]

    where:

    \[ e(t) = r(t) - y(t) \]
    • \(r(t)\) = reference or desired value
    • \(y(t)\) = actual output
    • \(e(t)\) = error
    • \(u(t)\) = control input

    Intuition¶

    A PID controller uses three kinds of information:

    • Present error → Proportional term
    • Past error → Integral term
    • Future trend of error → Derivative term

    So PID is powerful because it does not only look at what is happening now.
    It also considers what has been happening and what is likely to happen next.

    3) Proportional Control (P)¶

    The proportional controller uses only the current error:

    \[ u_P(t) = K_p e(t) \]

    Meaning¶

    If the error becomes larger, the control action also becomes larger.

    If the error is small, the control action is small.

    So proportional control is the most direct and intuitive form of control.

    Example: Self-Balancing Robot¶

    Suppose a balancing robot tilts by some angle.

    • If the tilt is small, apply small motor correction
    • If the tilt is large, apply stronger motor correction

    That means the motor command is proportional to the tilt error.

    Why this example fits¶

    This example fits well because: - the relationship between tilt and correction is direct - students can easily visualize it - it connects naturally to robotics

    Advantage¶

    • simple
    • fast reaction
    • easy to implement

    Limitation¶

    Proportional control alone often cannot remove steady-state error.

    That means the output may come close to the target, but not exactly reach it.

    4) Integral Control (I)¶

    The integral controller uses the accumulated error over time:

    \[ u_I(t) = K_i \int_0^t e(\tau)\, d\tau \]

    Meaning¶

    If a small error remains for a long time, the integral term keeps growing.

    So even if the present error is small, the controller remembers that the system has been wrong for a while.

    That is why integral action helps remove steady-state error.

    Example: Motor Speed Control¶

    Suppose the desired speed is:

    \[ r(t) = 10 \text{ rad/s} \]

    but the motor settles at:

    \[ y(t) = 9.5 \text{ rad/s} \]

    Then the error is:

    \[ e(t) = 10 - 9.5 = 0.5 \]

    If this error stays for a long time, the integral term accumulates it and increases the input until the output gets closer to the desired value.

    Why this example fits¶

    This example fits because: - it shows a small but persistent error - it explains why proportional control alone may be insufficient - it naturally motivates the need for memory of past error

    Advantage¶

    • removes steady-state error

    Limitation¶

    Too much integral action may cause: - oscillation - slower settling - instability

    5) Derivative Control (D)¶

    The derivative controller uses the rate of change of error:

    \[ u_D(t) = K_d \frac{de(t)}{dt} \]

    Meaning¶

    Derivative action looks at how quickly the error is changing.

    • If the error is changing rapidly, derivative action reacts strongly
    • If the error is changing slowly, derivative action is small

    So derivative control works like a predictor or damping term.

    Example: Car Braking¶

    Suppose a car is approaching the target speed very quickly.

    Even if the current error is still not zero, the derivative term can detect that the error is dropping fast and reduce the control effort.

    This helps avoid overshoot.

    Why this example fits¶

    This example fits because: - it gives strong intuition for "predicting the trend" - students understand braking and damping naturally - it shows why derivative can smooth the response

    Advantage¶

    • reduces overshoot
    • improves damping
    • helps stabilize response

    Limitation¶

    Derivative action is sensitive to noise.

    Because noise can change rapidly, the derivative term may amplify it.

    That is why derivative control is rarely used alone.

    6) Full PID Controller¶

    Combining all three terms:

    \[ u(t) = K_p e(t) + K_i \int_0^t e(\tau)\, d\tau + K_d \frac{de(t)}{dt} \]

    Each term plays a different role:

    Term Role
    \(K_p\) reacts to present error
    \(K_i\) removes persistent error
    \(K_d\) reduces overshoot and improves damping

    Example: Drone Attitude Control¶

    A drone is disturbed by wind.

    • P reacts immediately to the tilt error
    • I removes small long-term drift
    • D prevents oscillation and smooths the motion

    Why this example fits¶

    This is a very good example because: - drones need fast correction - they must remain accurate - too much oscillation is dangerous

    So all three PID terms become meaningful together.

    7) Effect of Increasing Each Gain¶

    Increase \(K_p\)¶

    Usually: - rise time decreases - response becomes faster - overshoot may increase

    Increase \(K_i\)¶

    Usually: - steady-state error decreases - accuracy improves - overshoot may increase - settling may become slower

    Increase \(K_d\)¶

    Usually: - overshoot decreases - damping improves - settling may improve - sensitivity to noise increases

    8) Small PID Math Example¶

    Consider a simple error signal at some instant:

    \[ e(t) = 2 \]

    Suppose:

    \[ \int_0^t e(\tau)\, d\tau = 5 \]

    and

    \[ \frac{de(t)}{dt} = -0.4 \]

    Let the gains be:

    \[ K_p = 3, \quad K_i = 0.5, \quad K_d = 1.2 \]

    Then the PID output is:

    \[ u(t) = K_p e(t) + K_i \int_0^t e(\tau)\, d\tau + K_d \frac{de(t)}{dt} \]

    Substitute the values:

    \[ u(t) = 3(2) + 0.5(5) + 1.2(-0.4) \]
    \[ u(t) = 6 + 2.5 - 0.48 \]
    \[ u(t) = 8.02 \]

    Interpretation¶

    • The proportional term contributes most because current error is significant
    • The integral term adds extra correction because past error has accumulated
    • The derivative term subtracts a little because the error is already decreasing

    This is exactly how PID balances fast response, accuracy, and damping.

    9) Simple Control Example with a Plant¶

    Suppose the plant is approximately:

    \[ G(s) = \frac{1}{s+1} \]

    If we use a proportional controller only:

    \[ C(s) = K_p \]

    then the open-loop transfer function becomes:

    \[ L(s) = C(s)G(s) = \frac{K_p}{s+1} \]

    For unity feedback, the closed-loop transfer function is:

    \[ T(s) = \frac{L(s)}{1+L(s)} \]

    So,

    \[ T(s) = \frac{\frac{K_p}{s+1}}{1+\frac{K_p}{s+1}} = \frac{K_p}{s+1+K_p} \]

    What this shows¶

    If \(K_p\) increases, the system generally responds faster.

    But in more complex systems, increasing \(K_p\) too much can cause oscillation or instability.

    This is why tuning matters.

    10) Ziegler–Nichols Tuning¶

    One classical way to tune PID is the Ziegler–Nichols closed-loop method.

    Idea¶

    1. Set: $$ K_i = 0, \quad K_d = 0 $$

    2. Increase \(K_p\) gradually until the system produces sustained oscillation

    3. Record:

    4. \(K_u\) = ultimate gain
    5. \(P_u\) = oscillation period

    Then use formulas to choose \(K_p\), \(K_i\), and \(K_d\).

    11) Ziegler–Nichols Rules¶

    P Controller¶

    \[ K_p = 0.5 K_u \]

    PI Controller¶

    \[ K_p = 0.45 K_u \]
    \[ T_i = \frac{P_u}{1.2} \]
    \[ K_i = \frac{K_p}{T_i} \]

    PID Controller¶

    \[ K_p = 0.6 K_u \]
    \[ T_i = \frac{P_u}{2} \]
    \[ T_d = \frac{P_u}{8} \]

    and then:

    \[ K_i = \frac{K_p}{T_i}, \qquad K_d = K_p T_d \]

    12) Small Ziegler–Nichols Example¶

    Suppose after testing the system, you find:

    \[ K_u = 20, \qquad P_u = 4 \text{ s} \]

    For PID tuning:

    \[ K_p = 0.6K_u = 0.6(20) = 12 \]
    \[ T_i = \frac{P_u}{2} = \frac{4}{2} = 2 \]
    \[ T_d = \frac{P_u}{8} = \frac{4}{8} = 0.5 \]

    Now compute:

    \[ K_i = \frac{K_p}{T_i} = \frac{12}{2} = 6 \]
    \[ K_d = K_p T_d = 12(0.5) = 6 \]

    So the tuned PID gains become:

    \[ K_p = 12, \qquad K_i = 6, \qquad K_d = 6 \]

    Why this section is useful¶

    Students often hear that PID tuning is important, but they do not know how to begin.

    Ziegler–Nichols gives them: - a practical starting method - a repeatable procedure - a connection between experiment and controller design

    13) Limitations of Ziegler–Nichols¶

    Ziegler–Nichols is useful, but not perfect.

    It may produce: - aggressive tuning - overshoot - noisy behavior in real systems

    So in practice, engineers often: - start with Ziegler–Nichols - then fine-tune manually

    14) Final Summary¶

    PID combines: - present error - past error - future trend

    That is why it works so well.

    Key Takeaways¶

    • P gives fast reaction
    • I removes steady-state error
    • D improves damping and reduces overshoot
    • PID is powerful because it combines all three
    • Ziegler–Nichols provides a practical tuning method

    Self Check Quiz¶

    Part A — Conceptual MCQ¶

    Q1¶

    Which PID term reacts to the current error?

    A) Integral
    B) Derivative
    C) Proportional
    D) Sensor

    Answer: C

    Q2¶

    Which PID term helps remove steady-state error?

    A) P
    B) I
    C) D
    D) None

    Answer: B

    Q3¶

    Which PID term is most sensitive to measurement noise?

    A) P
    B) I
    C) D
    D) All equally

    Answer: C

    Q4¶

    If a system responds too slowly, which gain is usually increased first?

    A) \(K_p\)
    B) \(K_i\)
    C) \(K_d\)
    D) Sensor gain

    Answer: A

    Q5¶

    If a system has too much overshoot, which term is often useful?

    A) P
    B) I
    C) D
    D) Input reference

    Answer: C

    Q6¶

    In Ziegler–Nichols tuning, which values are measured experimentally?

    A) \(K_p\) and \(K_i\)
    B) \(K_u\) and \(P_u\)
    C) \(K_d\) and \(T_d\)
    D) rise time and settling time

    Answer: B

    Q7¶

    What does the integral term do when a small error stays for a long time?

    A) ignores it
    B) amplifies noise only
    C) accumulates it
    D) cancels derivative action

    Answer: C

    Q8¶

    Why is derivative action usually not used alone?

    A) It is too slow
    B) It cannot handle noise well
    C) It removes all error automatically
    D) It does not depend on error

    Answer: B

    Part B — Short Questions¶

    Q1¶

    What is the main purpose of PID control?

    Answer:
    To improve system response by reducing error using present, past, and future error information.

    Q2¶

    Why is proportional control alone often insufficient?

    Answer:
    Because it may leave steady-state error.

    Q3¶

    What is steady-state error?

    Answer:
    It is the difference between desired output and actual output after the system has settled.

    Q4¶

    Why can integral action cause oscillation?

    Answer:
    Because it accumulates past error and may apply excessive correction.

    Q5¶

    What is the main role of derivative action?

    Answer:
    To predict the trend of error and reduce overshoot.

    Q6¶

    What is \(K_u\) in Ziegler–Nichols tuning?

    Answer:
    It is the ultimate gain, the proportional gain at which sustained oscillation begins.

    Q7¶

    What is \(P_u\) in Ziegler–Nichols tuning?

    Answer:
    It is the oscillation period measured at the ultimate gain.

    Q8¶

    Why is PID widely used in robotics?

    Answer:
    Because it gives a practical balance of speed, accuracy, and stability.

    Part C — Thinking Questions¶

    Q1¶

    Can a system be fast but still inaccurate?

    Answer:
    Yes. A system may respond quickly but still have steady-state error.

    Q2¶

    Why is tuning necessary even if the PID formula is known?

    Answer:
    Because different systems behave differently, so the gains must be adjusted to match the plant.

    Q3¶

    Why is Ziegler–Nichols only a starting point?

    Answer:
    Because it often gives aggressive gains, so practical systems usually need further refinement.

    Key Idea¶

    A good controller is not just mathematically correct.
    It must also match the behavior that the real system needs.

    That is why understanding the meaning of P, I, and D is just as important as knowing the formula.
