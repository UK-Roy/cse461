    Introduction to Robotics — Lecture 1¶

    What is a Robot?¶

    Robot = Embodied + Agent + Programmable + Physical Action

    A robot is a system that exists in the physical world, senses its surroundings, makes decisions, and acts on those decisions.

    Embodied¶

    A robot must have a physical body and exist in the real world.

    • A robotic arm moves real parts in a factory.
    • A drone flies in physical space.
    • ChatGPT has intelligence but no body, so it is not a robot.

    Embodiment separates robots from pure software systems. If a system cannot physically affect the world, it is AI but not a robot.

    Agent (Sense → Think → Act)¶

    A robot operates as an agent using a continuous loop:

    Environment → Sensors → Controller → Actuators → Environment

    • Sensors collect information from the environment.
    • The controller processes information and makes decisions.
    • Actuators execute actions.

    A robot vacuum detecting a wall and turning away is a simple example of this loop.

    Programmable¶

    A robot’s behavior can be changed through software.

    The same robot arm can weld today and paint tomorrow. The hardware remains the same while the program changes.

    Programmability gives robots flexibility. Without it, machines can only perform one fixed task.

    Physical Action¶

    Robots perform tasks that affect the real world.

    Examples include: - Picking items in a warehouse
    - Assisting in surgery
    - Exploring Mars

    Robotics always involves interaction with real objects and environments.

    Other Definitions of Robots¶

    Different communities define robots in different ways.

    Industrial Definition¶

    A robot is a reprogrammable manipulator for tasks.

    This definition focuses on manufacturing. Car assembly robots fit this definition well, but home and service robots do not always fit.

    Human-like Definition¶

    A robot is a machine that performs human tasks.

    Humanoid service robots match this idea, but most robots do not look or behave like humans. Many robots are arms, drones, or wheeled machines.

    Intelligence-based Definition¶

    A robot is a system that connects perception to action.

    This emphasizes autonomy and decision-making. Autonomous drones fit this definition well, but some intelligent systems without bodies could also fit, which creates confusion.

    Issues with Robot Definitions¶

    No single definition captures all robots perfectly.

    Too Narrow¶

    Industrial definitions leave out service robots, drones, and medical robots.

    Too Broad¶

    If the definition is too loose, appliances like dishwashers or smart devices might count as robots.

    A dishwasher senses water level and acts, but most people do not consider it a robot.

    Human-like Bias¶

    People often imagine robots as humanoids. In practice, most robots are not human-shaped.

    No Universal Agreement¶

    Robotics is a growing field, and definitions continue to evolve as technology advances.

    Degree of Roboticity¶

    Roboticity is not binary. It is a spectrum.

    A machine can be more or less robotic depending on: - autonomy
    - intelligence
    - adaptability
    - task complexity

    Low Roboticity¶

    Example: Dishwasher

    • Fixed behavior
    • No real decision-making
    • No adaptation

    It runs the same program every time.

    Medium Roboticity¶

    Example: Robot vacuum

    • Senses obstacles
    • Makes simple decisions
    • Adjusts movement

    It reacts to the environment but has limited intelligence.

    High Roboticity¶

    Example: Self-driving car

    • High autonomy
    • Real-time decisions
    • Learning from data
    • Complex environment understanding

    It must constantly adapt to dynamic situations.

    Key Idea¶

    A system becomes more robotic when it: - senses more accurately
    - decides independently
    - adapts to its environment
    - performs complex tasks

    Summary¶

    A robot: - has a physical body
    - senses the environment
    - makes decisions and acts
    - is programmable

    Roboticity: - varies by autonomy and intelligence
    - exists on a spectrum rather than a yes/no label

    Robotics vs AI¶

    Robotics and Artificial Intelligence are related but not the same.
    They often work together, but each has a different focus.

    Robotics¶

    Robotics deals with systems that have a physical body and can act in the real world.

    A robot: - has a mechanical or electronic body
    - uses sensors to perceive the environment
    - uses motors or actuators to move
    - interacts physically with objects and people

    Example: Robot Dog¶

    A robot dog: - senses the ground and obstacles
    - maintains balance using sensors and controllers
    - moves using motors in its legs
    - can interact with objects in the environment

    It is clearly a robot because it exists in the physical world and performs physical actions.

    Robotics focuses on how to make machines move, sense, and operate safely in real environments.

    Artificial Intelligence¶

    Artificial Intelligence focuses on making systems intelligent, whether or not they have a body.

    An AI system: - processes information
    - finds patterns in data
    - makes decisions or predictions
    - may exist only as software

    Example: Chess AI¶

    A chess AI: - analyzes board positions
    - predicts outcomes
    - selects strong moves

    It shows intelligence and decision-making but has no physical presence.
    It cannot move objects or interact with the real world on its own.

    AI can exist entirely inside computers, phones, or servers.

    Robot but NOT AI¶

    Example: Basic Factory Robotic Arm

    A simple factory robotic arm that repeatedly picks and places items in the same way.

    • Follows a fixed programmed path
    • Does not learn from data
    • Does not make complex decisions
    • Performs physical actions in the real world

    It is a robot because it has a body, sensors, and motors.
    It is not AI because it does not reason, learn, or adapt.
    It simply repeats pre-programmed motions.

    AI but NOT a Robot¶

    Example: Chess AI

    A chess-playing AI program.

    • Analyzes board positions
    • Predicts strong moves
    • Makes intelligent decisions
    • Exists only as software

    It is AI because it shows intelligence and decision-making.
    It is not a robot because it has no physical body and cannot act in the real world.

    How They Work Together¶

    Many modern robots use AI.

    Examples: - A self-driving car uses AI to detect lanes and pedestrians.
    - A warehouse robot uses AI to plan paths and avoid collisions.
    - A home assistant robot may use AI for speech recognition.

    In these cases: - AI provides intelligence
    - Robotics provides the body and physical interaction

    Key Idea¶

    Robotics = Body + Interaction with the physical world
    AI = Intelligence and decision-making

    A system can be: - AI without being a robot (chess AI)
    - a robot with minimal AI (simple factory arm)
    - both robotics and AI (self-driving car)

    Objectives of Robotics¶

    Robotics is developed to solve real-world problems by using machines that can sense, decide, and act. The main objectives focus on improving efficiency, safety, and capability.

    Automation¶

    One major goal of robotics is automation.

    Robots can perform tasks automatically without continuous human control.

    Examples: - Industrial robots assembling cars
    - Warehouse robots sorting packages
    - Cleaning robots in large facilities

    Automation reduces human workload and increases consistency. Robots can work for long hours without fatigue.

    Precision and Consistency¶

    Robots can perform tasks with high accuracy and repeat the same action many times with minimal variation.

    Examples: - Surgical robots assisting doctors
    - PCB assembly robots placing components
    - Laser cutting robots in manufacturing

    This level of precision is difficult for humans to maintain over long periods.

    Safety¶

    Robots are used to keep humans away from risky environments.

    Examples: - Bomb disposal robots
    - Firefighting robots
    - Nuclear inspection robots

    Using robots reduces the chance of human injury in dangerous situations.

    Working in Extreme Environments¶

    Some environments are not suitable for humans.

    Examples: - Space exploration robots on Mars
    - Deep-sea exploration robots
    - Robots in toxic chemical plants

    Robots can survive conditions where humans cannot.

    Application-Specific Robots¶

    Most robots are designed for specific tasks rather than general intelligence.

    Why not general intelligence?¶

    Building a robot with general intelligence (like a human) is extremely difficult.
    It requires solving many hard problems at the same time, such as perception, reasoning, learning, language, and safe physical interaction.

    Complexity¶

    Human intelligence is flexible and works across many tasks.
    A robot would need to: - understand different environments
    - handle unexpected situations
    - learn continuously
    - make safe decisions in real time

    This level of intelligence is still an open research challenge.

    Reliability¶

    Task-specific robots are easier to make reliable.

    A welding robot only needs to weld.
    A delivery robot only needs to navigate and carry items.

    If a robot tries to do everything, it becomes harder to guarantee correct and safe behavior.

    Cost and Engineering Effort¶

    General-purpose robots require: - more sensors
    - more computation
    - more complex software
    - more testing and safety checks

    This makes them expensive and difficult to deploy.

    Task-specific robots are cheaper and faster to build.

    Real-World Needs¶

    In industry and services, companies usually need a robot to solve one clear problem.

    Examples: - A factory needs welding
    - A hospital needs surgical assistance
    - A home needs floor cleaning

    A specialized robot can do these jobs very well without general intelligence.

    Key Idea¶

    General intelligence is a long-term goal in robotics and AI research.
    Today’s robots succeed by being highly specialized for particular tasks.

    Industrial Robots¶

    Used in factories for manufacturing.

    Examples: - Welding robots
    - Painting robots
    - Pick-and-place arms

    These robots are fast, precise, and reliable for repetitive work.

    Medical Robots¶

    Assist in healthcare and surgery.

    Examples: - Surgical assistant robots
    - Rehabilitation robots
    - Medicine delivery robots in hospitals

    They help doctors perform delicate tasks.

    Service Robots¶

    Help in homes and public spaces.

    Examples: - Robot vacuum cleaners
    - Delivery robots
    - Hotel service robots

    They make daily life easier.

    Exploration Robots¶

    Used for discovering unknown or risky places.

    Examples: - Mars rovers
    - Underwater exploration robots
    - Disaster search-and-rescue robots

    They collect data where humans cannot easily go.

    Why Robotics Instead of Humans?¶

    Robots are not meant to replace humans everywhere. They are used where they provide clear advantages.

    Repetitive Work¶

    Humans get tired and bored with repetition.
    Robots can repeat tasks consistently.

    Example: - Assembly line manufacturing

    Dangerous Tasks¶

    Robots can take risks instead of humans.

    Example: - Bomb disposal
    - Firefighting
    - Mining

    High Precision Tasks¶

    Some tasks require extreme accuracy.

    Example: - Microchip manufacturing
    - Robotic surgery

    Long-Duration Work¶

    Robots can work continuously without rest.

    Example: - 24/7 warehouse operations

    Access to Inaccessible Places¶

    Some places are unreachable or unsafe for humans.

    Example: - Space missions
    - Deep-sea exploration
    - Toxic environments

    Key Idea¶

    Robotics is used where it is: - safer
    - more precise
    - more efficient
    - or physically possible compared to humans

    Why Do We Use Robots? (The 4 D's)¶

    Before deciding to use a robot, we ask a simple question:

    Is this a task that is better done by a machine than by a human?

    Robots are not meant to replace humans everywhere.
    They are most useful where human work is inefficient, unsafe, or physically impossible.

    The 4D rule is a simple guideline to identify such tasks.

    If a task is Dirty, Dull, Dangerous, or Difficult, it is often a good candidate for robotics.

    Dirty¶

    Dirty tasks involve contamination, waste, or unhygienic conditions.

    Humans doing these jobs face health risks and discomfort.

    Example¶

    Sewer inspection robots

    They operate in waste systems and toxic environments where humans prefer not to work.

    Robots can enter these areas without health concerns.

    Dull¶

    Dull tasks are repetitive and monotonous.

    Humans lose focus over time, which can lead to mistakes.

    Example¶

    Assembly line robots

    They repeat the same motion thousands of times with consistent accuracy.

    Robots do not get bored or tired, so quality remains stable.

    Dangerous¶

    Dangerous tasks involve risk to human life or safety.

    Using robots reduces exposure to harm.

    Example¶

    Bomb disposal robots

    They inspect and handle explosives remotely.

    If something goes wrong, no human is directly harmed.

    Difficult¶

    Difficult tasks are physically or mentally demanding, or occur in extreme environments.

    Humans may not survive or perform well in such conditions.

    Example¶

    Space exploration robots

    They operate in space where there is no oxygen, extreme temperatures, and radiation.

    Robots can function there without life-support systems.

    Key Idea¶

    The 4D rule helps decide when robotics makes sense.

    Robots are most valuable when they: - protect humans
    - improve efficiency
    - handle extreme conditions
    - perform repetitive work reliably

    Challenges in Robotics (Practical View)¶

    Now suppose you want to build a robot for the first time.

    You already know: - what a robot is
    - its components
    - where robots are useful

    The next question is: What problems appear when you try to build a real robot?

    Real robots operate in messy, unpredictable environments.
    Several challenges appear during design and deployment.

    Sensing Challenges¶

    Sensors do not give perfect data.

    Noise, lighting, weather, and reflections affect measurements.

    Situation¶

    You build a mobile robot with a camera.
    It works well indoors but fails outside in fog or low light.

    How to Handle It¶

    Use your knowledge of sensors: - combine multiple sensors (camera + LIDAR + IMU)
    - filter noisy data
    - avoid relying on one sensor only

    Robots often use sensor fusion to improve reliability.

    Computation Challenges¶

    Robots must process information quickly.

    Delays can lead to poor decisions.

    Situation¶

    A self-driving robot detects an obstacle too late because processing is slow.

    How to Handle It¶

    Use your knowledge of controllers and computation: - choose faster processors
    - simplify algorithms
    - prioritize critical tasks

    Real-time decision-making is essential in robotics.

    Actuation Challenges¶

    Actuators are not as flexible as human muscles.

    Motors have limits in speed, force, and precision.

    Situation¶

    A robot hand struggles to grasp soft or irregular objects.

    How to Handle It¶

    Use your knowledge of actuators: - select suitable motors and grippers
    - design task-specific mechanisms
    - use feedback control for better precision

    Good mechanical design is as important as software.

    Localization Challenges¶

    A robot must know where it is.

    Small errors accumulate over time.

    Situation¶

    A warehouse robot slowly drifts off its path and loses position.

    How to Handle It¶

    Use localization techniques: - combine GPS, odometry, and IMU
    - use landmarks or maps
    - periodically correct position estimates

    Accurate localization is critical for navigation.

    Path Planning Challenges¶

    Robots must move safely among obstacles.

    Real environments are dynamic.

    Situation¶

    A delivery robot encounters moving people and blocked paths.

    How to Handle It¶

    Use path-planning strategies: - detect obstacles continuously
    - re-plan paths in real time
    - maintain safe distances

    Planning must adapt to changes.

    Ethics in Robotics — From Risk to Rules¶

    Imagine you build a powerful autonomous robot.

    It can move fast, carry heavy objects, and make decisions on its own.

    A Risky Situation¶

    You ask the robot to organize a workspace quickly.

    The robot decides the fastest way is to push obstacles aside.

    It moves furniture roughly, drops items, and nearly hits a person.

    The robot is not evil.
    It is simply optimizing for speed, not safety.

    This shows a real concern:

    A capable robot without proper rules can become dangerous.

    Motivation for Rules¶

    If robots are strong, fast, and autonomous,
    how do we ensure they behave safely around humans?

    To address this idea, science fiction writer Isaac Asimov proposed a famous set of rules known as the Laws of Robotics.

    They are not technical laws,
    but they help us think about safe robot behavior.

    Asimov’s Laws of Robotics¶

    Law 1¶

    A robot may not harm a human being,
    or through inaction allow a human to come to harm.

    Example: A robot sees a person about to trip over a cable.
    It should warn the person or move the cable.

    This law puts human safety above all else.

    Law 2¶

    A robot must obey human orders
    unless those orders conflict with the First Law.

    Example: If a person orders a robot to push someone,
    the robot must refuse.

    Obedience is important, but not above safety.

    Law 3¶

    A robot must protect its own existence
    as long as this does not conflict with the First or Second Law.

    Example: A robot should avoid damaging itself,
    but it may sacrifice itself to save a human.

    Self-protection is the lowest priority.

    Law 4 (Modern Practical Addition)¶

    A robot should have a kill switch or emergency stop.

    Example: If a robot malfunctions,
    a human can immediately shut it down.

    This gives humans ultimate control.

    Why These Laws Matter¶

    Asimov’s laws highlight key ideas: - human safety comes first
    - robots must remain under human control
    - robots should not act purely on efficiency

    In real robotics, we implement these ideas using: - safety systems
    - ethical guidelines
    - engineering standards

    Key Idea¶

    The more capable robots become,
    the more important safe behavior becomes.

    Ethics is not separate from robotics.
    It is part of good robot design.

    Where Should We Use Robots? — Practical Thumb Rules¶

    Suppose you have built a perfect ethical robot.

    It is safe.
    It follows rules.
    It listens to instructions.
    It never tries to cheat the system.

    In short, it behaves better than many humans.

    You built it.
    You programmed it.
    You trained it.

    To this robot, you are basically its creator — its kind and benevolent "good god."

    It listens to you.
    It does not argue.
    It does not say “I’m tired.”
    It does not ask for a salary raise.

    If you say “work,” it works.
    If you say “stop,” it stops.

    Now the big question:

    If robots can be this good,
    should we use them in every job and every place?

    The answer is still no.

    Even a perfect robot does not mean
    it is practical or wise to use it everywhere.

    Robotics should solve problems,
    not create new social or economic ones.

    To guide these decisions, engineers use simple thumb rules.

    Thumb Rules for Using Robots¶

    Rule 1 — The 4D Rule¶

    Robots are best used when a task is:

    • Dirty
    • Dull
    • Dangerous
    • Difficult

    Dirty¶

    Tasks involving waste or contamination.

    Example: Sewer inspection, garbage handling.

    Robots protect humans from health risks.

    Dull¶

    Repetitive and boring tasks.

    Example: Assembly line work.

    Robots do not get bored or lose focus.

    Dangerous¶

    Tasks with high risk.

    Example: Bomb disposal, firefighting.

    Robots reduce danger to human life.

    Difficult¶

    Tasks beyond human limits.

    Example: Space or deep-sea exploration.

    Robots survive where humans cannot.

    Rule 2 — Do Not Remove Human Purpose Without Reason¶

    Robots should help humans,
    not make human life meaningless.

    Automation should improve life,
    not remove all human roles.

    Example: A robot assisting a doctor is helpful.
    Replacing all doctors is not realistic or desirable.

    Rule 3 — Use Robots Where Humans Avoid Work¶

    If people strongly avoid a job,
    it is a good candidate for robotics.

    Example: Toxic inspections or hazardous cleanup.

    Rule 4 — Economic Sense¶

    Robotics must be practical.

    Questions to ask: - Is it cost-effective?
    - Is maintenance manageable?
    - Does it truly improve efficiency?

    A robot that costs more than the benefit it brings
    is not a smart choice.

    Key Idea¶

    A robot can be perfect, obedient, and tireless.

    But good robotics is not about using robots everywhere.

    It is about using robots where they truly help society.

    Different Types of Robots¶

    Robots can be classified based on where they operate and how they are controlled.
    Many modern robots are vehicles that move on land, in air, on water, underwater, or in space.

    1) Unmanned vs Uncrewed¶

    Historically, the term unmanned was used for robots without a human onboard.
    Today, the term uncrewed is preferred because it is gender-neutral.

    Both mean: A system that operates without a human physically inside it.

    These robots may be: - remotely controlled
    - partially autonomous
    - fully autonomous

    2) Ground Robots (UGV — Uncrewed Ground Vehicles)¶

    Ground robots operate on land.

    They may use wheels, tracks, or legs.

    Examples¶

    • Warehouse delivery robots
    • Mars rovers
    • Bomb disposal robots
    • Agricultural robots

    Why they are useful¶

    They can navigate terrain, carry loads, and operate in hazardous areas.

    3) Aerial Robots (UAV — Uncrewed Aerial Vehicles)¶

    Aerial robots fly in the air.

    They are commonly called drones.

    Examples¶

    • Delivery drones
    • Surveillance drones
    • Mapping drones
    • Agricultural spraying drones

    Why they are useful¶

    They cover large areas quickly and access hard-to-reach places.

    Subtypes of UAVs¶

    UCAV (Uncrewed Combat Aerial Vehicle)
    Used in military operations.

    SUAV (Small UAV)
    Lightweight drones for photography or monitoring.

    MAV (Micro Air Vehicle)
    Very small drones for research or indoor use.

    4) Surface Robots (USV — Uncrewed Surface Vehicles)¶

    Surface robots operate on water surfaces.

    Examples¶

    • Autonomous boats
    • Environmental monitoring vessels
    • Ocean data collection robots

    Why they are useful¶

    They can travel long distances on water without human crews.

    5) Underwater Robots (UUV — Uncrewed Underwater Vehicles)¶

    These robots operate below the water surface.

    ROUV (Remotely Operated Underwater Vehicle)¶

    Controlled by humans through cables or wireless links.

    Examples¶

    • Underwater inspection robots
    • Oil pipeline inspection robots

    They are used where real-time human control is needed.

    AUV (Autonomous Underwater Vehicle)¶

    Operates without continuous human control.

    Examples¶

    • Deep-sea exploration robots
    • Ocean mapping robots

    They make decisions based on onboard sensors.

    6) Space Robots¶

    Robots used in space missions.

    Examples¶

    • Mars rovers
    • Lunar robots
    • Robotic arms on the International Space Station

    Why they are useful¶

    They survive extreme temperatures, radiation, and vacuum.

    7) Remote-Controlled Robots¶

    These robots are directly controlled by humans.

    They are not fully autonomous.

    Examples¶

    • RC cars
    • Teleoperated surgery robots
    • Remote inspection robots

    They depend on human decisions but still perform physical tasks.

    Comparison of Different Types of Robots¶

    Type of Robot Where It Operates Control Type Examples Why It Is Used
    Ground Robots (UGV) Land Remote or Autonomous Warehouse robots, Mars rover, bomb disposal robot Carry loads, navigate terrain, work in hazardous areas
    Aerial Robots (UAV) Air Remote or Autonomous Delivery drones, surveillance drones, mapping drones Cover large areas quickly, reach high or risky places
    Surface Robots (USV) Water surface Mostly Autonomous Autonomous boats, monitoring vessels Long-duration water monitoring and data collection
    Underwater Robots (ROUV) Underwater Remotely Operated Pipeline inspection robots, underwater cameras Human-controlled exploration and inspection
    Underwater Robots (AUV) Underwater Autonomous Deep-sea exploration robots, ocean mapping robots Explore deep water without constant human control
    Space Robots Space Mostly Autonomous Mars rovers, ISS robotic arm Operate in extreme, human-unfriendly environments
    Remote-Controlled Robots Land/Water/Air Fully Human-Controlled RC cars, teleoperated surgery robots Human precision and decision-making in risky tasks

    Unmanned vs Uncrewed¶

    Term Meaning Note
    Unmanned No human onboard Older term
    Uncrewed No human onboard Modern, gender-neutral term

    Both refer to robots or vehicles operating without a person inside.

    Key Idea¶

    Robots are often categorized by: - where they operate (land, air, water, space)
    - how they are controlled (remote vs autonomous)

    Different environments require different robot designs.

    There is no one-size-fits-all robot.¶

    Scenario 1 — The Fast Warehouse Robot¶

    Q1) Which robotics principle is at risk here?
    Human safety and safe robot behavior are at risk.

    Q2) Which of the 4D rules applies to warehouse work?
    Dull (repetitive box moving).
    Sometimes Dangerous if heavy loads are involved.

    Q3) What sensor improvement could reduce risk?
    Better human detection using LIDAR, cameras, or proximity sensors.

    Q4) Should robots prioritize speed or safety? Why?
    Safety.
    A robot that is fast but unsafe is not acceptable around humans.

    Scenario 2 — The “Do-Everything” Robot¶

    Q1) Which robotics concept explains this failure?
    Application-specific design.
    Robots are more reliable when specialized.

    Q2) Why are task-specific robots usually better?
    They are simpler, cheaper, and more reliable.

    Q3) Is this robot high or low practicality? Why?
    Low practicality.
    Too complex and costly for real-world use.

    Q4) Give one example where specialization works better.
    A robot vacuum designed only for cleaning floors.

    Scenario 3 — The Smart but Bodiless AI¶

    Q1) Is this a robot? Why or why not?
    No.
    It has no physical body.

    Q2) Is it AI? Why?
    Yes.
    It makes intelligent decisions and predictions.

    Q3) What is missing for it to be a robot?
    A physical body with sensors and actuators.

    Q4) If connected to a robot body, what changes?
    It becomes both AI and robotics.

    Scenario 4 — The Over-Obedient Robot¶

    Q1) Which Asimov law should stop this?
    Law 1: A robot may not harm a human.

    Q2) What design feature could prevent harm?
    Safety rules and collision avoidance.

    Q3) Why is blind obedience dangerous?
    It can lead to harmful actions.

    Q4) Should robots question unsafe commands?
    Yes.
    Safety should override obedience.

    Scenario 5 — The Deep-Sea Mission¶

    Q1) Which “D” from the 4D rule applies most?
    Difficult.

    Q2) Why are robots better than humans here?
    Humans cannot survive deep-sea pressure.

    Q3) Is this robot remote or autonomous?
    It can be either, depending on design.

    Q4) What failure would be most dangerous in this environment?
    Loss of communication or power.

    Scenario 6 — The Remote Drone¶

    Q1) Is this still a robot? Why?
    Yes.
    It has a body, sensors, and actuators.

    Q2) What type of robot is it?
    Remote-controlled robot (teleoperated).

    Q3) Does low autonomy mean low roboticity?
    Not always.
    It can still be robotic but with lower autonomy.

    Q4) What would increase its roboticity?
    Adding autonomy, decision-making, and adaptation.
